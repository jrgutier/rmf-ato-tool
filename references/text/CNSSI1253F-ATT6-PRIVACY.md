Privacy Overlays

# 1. Purpose and Scope

The Privacy Overlays support the Categorize and Select steps of the National Institute of
Standards, and Technology (NIST) Risk Management Framework (RMF). The Privacy Overlays
identify security and privacy control specifications required to protect Personally Identifiable
Information (PII) in National Security Systems (NSS) and reduce privacy risks to individuals,
agencies, and organizations throughout the information lifecycle. The Privacy Overlays do not
supersede or change privacy requirements established by statutes, regulations, Executive Orders
(EO), or applicable Federal policies or alter any agency authorities. Where certain activities are
not required for NSS by relevant authorities, the Privacy Overlays may still require the activities
for NSS to reduce privacy risk levels at as matter of policy. Agencies have significant flexibility
in implementing the RMF, including these Privacy Overlays, as long as they are meeting all
applicable requirements and effectively managing security and privacy risk.
Systems used for activities under EO 12333, both classified and unclassified, process PII,
including United States Persons Information (USPI) about U.S. citizens and lawful resident
aliens, as well as information about foreign nationals.1 The Privacy Act of 1974, as amended,
(hereinafter Privacy Act) protects records about U.S. citizens and lawful resident aliens
(collectively called U.S. Persons, or USP) maintained in systems of records. Additional policies
protect PII about U.S. citizens, lawful permanent residents, and foreign nationals.2 Therefore,
NSS must apply the Privacy Overlays to protect PII about USP and foreign nationals.3 Note,
applying the Privacy Overlays protects PII in NSS but does not create new legal obligations nor
grant any new rights or privileges to individuals that did not already exist.
NSS administrators will consult with the SAOP, or their designees, and may consult with agency
legal counsel, through a system-specific analyses of the relevant privacy risks to individuals and
organizations, as well as the underlying legal, policy, and regulatory authorities, to determine the
complete list of privacy controls that are applicable to an individual NSS that processes PII. NSS
administrators also should review the privacy controls and make any tailoring determinations.4
Tailoring is a critical activity to ensure informed risk management decisions reflect safeguards
needed to support ability to execute its mission.

The Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs)5 inform Federal statutes and laws, including the
Privacy Act, and are critical to how the government approaches information management,
including the processing of PII. Implementing the FIPPS, the Privacy Act established the
requirement for “appropriate administrative, technical, and physical safeguards to ensure the
security and confidentiality of records” and to “protect... the integrity” of systems. 6 Fifty years
after the establishment of the Privacy Act, as technology and threats to technology evolve,
organizations continue to modernize how they protect information. NIST Special Publication
(SP) 800-53 provides the current catalog of security and privacy controls for federal systems to
protect information.
CNSSI No. 1253 Appendix D Tables D-1 through D-20 establish a Privacy Implementation
Considerations column, which identifies controls that are not included in the privacy control
baseline but may introduce privacy risk if selected to meet security objectives. CNSSI No. 1253
requires that the privacy control baseline must be selected regardless of whether or not the
system processes PII. The Privacy Overlays complement the privacy control baseline by
identifying additional controls that must be selected for systems that create, collect, use, process,
store, maintain, disseminate, disclose, or dispose of (collectively referred to as “process”)7 PII
based on the privacy risk for the system as discussed in this document. System owners must
consult with the Senior Agency Official for Privacy (SAOP), or designee to determine the
complete list of applicable privacy controls and privacy requirements for an NSS that processes
PII.
Building privacy safeguards into IT systems helps prevent the loss, breach, misuse, or abuse of
data, or processing data in unintended ways. As new programs are developed and old programs
renewed, systematically identifying the PII in the system, evaluating privacy risks associated
with processing the PII and mitigating those risks are instrumental to systems and programs that
serve the Federal mission and public. Security and privacy professionals work collaboratively to
support organizations and programs with applying the Privacy Overlays.
The Privacy Overlays identify the security and privacy controls and specifications that help
organizations manage privacy risk and address the privacy requirements of federal statutes,
regulations, policies, and standards. The Privacy Overlays also incorporate a foundational control
set composed of controls needed for systems that process PII for purposes of maintaining audit
logs and account management records, such as usernames, access permissions, roles, group
affiliation, etc.
In addition to identifying controls that protect PII, the Privacy Overlays provide guidance for
identifying the overall privacy risk level of a system based on impacts to the individuals whose
PII is processed by the system, as well as the related risk to the organization. The privacy risk
level (low, moderate, or high) is the product of the magnitude of potential harm or negative

Privacy and security controls selected to manage privacy risk in information systems require an
independent analysis, distinct from that required for dissemination restrictions, e.g., “NOFORN”,
and security classifications, e.g., “Secret”. Security classifications focus on protecting national
security interests, while privacy controls manage privacy risk and focus on protecting individuals
and organizations from potential harms specific to processing PII. The system’s privacy risk
level is independent and separate from the system’s classification.8
PII and data processing activities in NSS may create various types of privacy risks resulting from
the loss, misuse, inappropriate access, or abuse of PII. To that end, many security measures also
support privacy needs. However, addressing security risk does not address all types of privacy
risk. Even when PII is protected against security threats and vulnerabilities, the system may still
present privacy risks because of its intended activities, or data actions, also referred to as
"problematic data actions”. A valid mission and business process that is necessary and
intentional can still introduce privacy risk by nature of the activity.9
The Privacy Overlays in this document provide a consistent approach for organizations to
identify “appropriate administrative, technical, and physical safeguards” to protect PII in systems
regardless of whether the system that maintains the PII is a system of records as defined by the
Privacy Act.10 While many of the controls in the Privacy Overlays are system-level controls,
agencies may choose to address some controls at the organizational level and allow systems to
inherit the controls when appropriate. Determinations of inheritability should be made on a case-by-case
basis specific to the structure and function of the organization and system.
The Privacy Overlays are one part of a larger ecosystem of privacy requirements. Controls
address or mitigate risks affecting confidentiality, integrity, and availability of PII. Additionally,
controls selected for the Privacy Overlays both mitigate and help identify potential privacy risks
other than those affecting confidentiality, integrity, and availability. One example, RA-8
(Privacy Impact Assessments), requires an assessment of privacy risk for NSS that have PII and
may identify problematic data actions that can be addressed through privacy engineering.
Another example, SR-4 (Provenance), requires maintaining an understanding of how PII moves
and changes throughout data processing activities and may identify privacy relevant changes to
the system.

3. Overlays Characteristics
3.1 Definition of PII

To protect PII within NSS, system owners must be able to identify PII and should recognize that
inclusion of PII in a system may not be immediately apparent. PII is defined in CNSSI No. 4009,
Glossary, and OMB Circular A-130 as, “information that can be used to distinguish or trace an
individual’s identity, either alone or when combined with other information that is linked or
linkable to a specific individual”. The definition of PII is not based on the location or nationality
of the individual. The definition of PII also is not limited to any single type of information or
technology. Rather, determining PII requires a case-by-case assessment of the specific likelihood
that an individual can be identified. PII may appear in various forms and contexts in NSS.
Practitioners must understand the data that is processed by their system or tool, including
whether PII will or may be processed, or if data may become PII after processing. Resources that
may help practitioners determine the PII their system processes or may process include the
system description, data dictionaries, data stores, architecture for the data store(s), and data flow
diagrams.
The instances in Sections 3.1.1 - 3.1.3 provide representative examples of data that are PII. These
examples are not exhaustive lists of the data elements that are PII, but rather show some of the
myriad ways data elements may be PII independently or can become PII in combination.
3.1.1 Data Elements that Alone Identify an Individual

<!-- page 8 -->

Data elements that alone can distinguish or trace an individual’s identity are also referred to as
direct identifiers. Direct identifiers are specific characteristics of an individual, such as
biometrics, or an identifier assigned to an individual, such as a name or employee identification
number. There is no minimum time threshold for a direct identifier to be assigned to an
individual for the identifier to be PII. Presence of a data element that is a direct identifier in a
system is PII, even if some of the discrete entries in that data field are incomplete, e.g., a
database of fingerprints where some entries only have a partial print. Data elements may also be
PII alone if the data element is assigned to a device associated with a specific individual, e.g.,
cell phone number or advertising identifier.
Instance #1: Direct Identifiers are PII.
Example:

• Name alone – This is true whether a name is common (e.g., Mike Smith), uncommon
(e.g., Glenn Schlarman), or a username (e.g., Peanut1000).
• Cell phone number.
• Personal identification numbers (e.g., social security number, passport number, employee
identification number, driver’s license number, personal financial account numbers, state
identification number, patient identification number, vehicle identification number).
• Email Address.
• Biometrics (e.g., facial image, fingerprint, iris, retina, voice prints, x-rays)
• Asset information or unique alphanumeric string uniquely assigned to an individual
device, person, or small well-defined group of people (e.g., Internet Protocol (IP) address
or Media Access Control (MAC) address, International Mobile Equipment Identity
(IMEI), advertising identifier).
3.1.2. Data Elements that in Combination Identify an Individual (Compilation)
Data elements that are indirect identifiers, or data that alone or without additional context does
not identify an individual, will become PII when combined with other data elements about that
individual or context whereby that combination is sufficient to enable identification. This is also
referred to as the compilation or mosaic effect. The mosaic effect occurs, and data becomes PII,
whether identification of individuals is inadvertent or intentional.
These combined data elements may be linked or associated within a single data repository or
system or may be linked or associated through data processing across multiple repositories, or
when the data is used for the purpose of identifying individuals within specific mission contexts.
Regardless of where or when this association occurs, it is important to evaluate if the association
results in the ability to identify an individual. Additionally, if the purpose of combining the data
elements is to determine the identity of the individual, then by definition the mosaic effect has
occurred, and the data is PII.

other words, when information that is not otherwise attributed to an individual is associated with
that individual’s PII, then the non-attributable information becomes PII by association.

## 3.2 Privacy Overlays Controls

The Privacy Overlays provide four baseline-independent control sets (Accountability, Low,
Moderate, and High) and a selection of individual optional controls (Privacy Enhancing) to
enable system owners to manage systems with varying levels of privacy risk, and support
compliance with federal privacy requirements.13 A privacy risk analysis must be completed for
all information systems that process PII to identify the applicable control set.14 The Privacy
Overlays incorporate an adapted version of the FIPS 199 impact values as adopted by CNSSI
No. 1253 to address NSS that process PII at a Low, Moderate, and High privacy risk level. The
Privacy Overlays also include an Accountability PII control set to address systems that, whether
or not they intentionally process PII, have access or use controls on the system that identify
individual users for accountability purposes (e.g., audit logs, user accesses for a particular data
set). Each control set layers additional protections required for the next higher risk level from
Accountability to High (e.g., the Low control set layers on controls on top of the Accountability
control set). Finally, the Privacy Overlays include a column with a Privacy Enhancing indicator
denoting optional controls that the organization may tailor in individually to address system-specific
privacy risks (e.g., controls related to de-identification can further enhance the privacy
risk posture of systems).
In the event that a control is required by both the security control baseline and the Privacy
Overlays, the control may be implemented in a manner that addresses both security and privacy
interests rather than requiring multiple iterations of the same control.

capture users’ access, activities, or processing of data in the system?
No: If the system does not process audit logs20 or account management records, then the ACCT
Overlay is not required, and the analysis is finished. The controls in the Privacy Control Baseline
are sufficient.
Yes: If the system does process audit logs or account management records, the Accountability
(ACCT) control set is required to protect this PII.

## 4.2 Privacy Overlays Relationship to Categorize and Select in the Risk

Management Framework

The Privacy Overlays correlate to Categorize and Select in the RMF. Previously, system owners
would select the PCIL for the PII in the system, and then consider the PCIL when determining
the impact value for the system’s security objectives. Conducting the privacy risk analysis using
this document results in a privacy risk level of low, moderate, or high for the system and replaces
the PCIL. Organizations should use this privacy risk level result in place of the PCIL.21

AC-2, ACCOUNT MANAGEMENT

Justification to Select for ACCT, Low, Moderate, and High: Account management is a
critical function for developing and implementing an access control framework that is
appropriate for the information contained in systems and applications. When
implemented effectively, the access control framework provides the necessary constructs
for controlling access to PII, limiting disclosure of records about individuals to only those
system and application users that have a need for the information to perform their job
functions. Organizations should provide access only to the minimum amount of PII
necessary for users to perform their duties.
Guidance: Use of temporary accounts is prohibited for accessing PII as these accounts
have increased risk and little or no accountability.

AC-3, ACCESS ENFORCEMENT

Justification to Select for ACCT, Low, Moderate, and High: Organizations shall control
access to PII through access enforcement mechanisms. Well-designed, automated access
controls (e.g., mandatory access control (MAC), discretionary access control (DAC),
role-based access control (RBAC), or attribute-based access control (ABAC)) limit user
access to information according to defined access policies, which helps ensure the
security and confidentiality of the PII processed by the system.
Guidance: For example, implement role-based access controls and configure access
controls so that each user can access only the pieces of information necessary for the
user’s role or only permit users to access PII through an application that restricts their
access to the PII the users require, instead of allowing users direct access to a database or
files containing PII.

Justification to Select for ACCT, Low, Moderate, and High: PII released outside a system

an external entity that was created using data extracted from a system.
Moderate and High Parameter Value: Release information outside of the system only if:

AC-21, INFORMATION SHARING

Justification to Select for ACCT, Low, Moderate, and High: PII protected by the Privacy

Implementation Considerations

There are two new tools that support collaboration between privacy and security offices
regarding privacy controls: the Privacy Implementation Considerations column in the CNSSI No.
1253 and the Collaboration Index for Security and Privacy Controls (Collaboration Index) which
is available through the Federal Privacy Council’s website FPC.gov.
CNSSI No. 1253 identified certain controls in its Privacy Implementation Considerations
column. Selection of these controls requires coordination with the SAOP, or designee, to address
any privacy risks and mitigation considerations that may arise from selecting controls listed
under privacy implementation considerations.
The Federal Privacy Council and the Federal Chief Information Officer Council jointly
developed the Collaboration Index to serve as a guide to help Federal information security
programs and Federal privacy programs consider the level of collaboration between the security
and privacy offices necessary for each control. For each control and control enhancement listed
in NIST Special Publication 800-53 Revision 5, the Collaboration Index provides a value that
denotes the level of collaboration that may be appropriate.
The Collaboration Index and the Privacy Implementation Considerations column in CNSSI 1253
have a different scope and purpose. The Privacy Implementation Considerations indicator
denotes the situation in which security programs should coordinate with privacy programs
because how the control is implemented may introduce unexpected privacy risk. This is distinct
from the Collaboration Index, which provides general guidance on the amount of collaboration
that may be appropriate between information security programs and privacy programs and is not
specific to NSS. If an organization does not already have established mechanisms for
collaboration to implement Privacy Implementation Consideration controls, the Collaboration
Index offers a starting point for determining the degree of responsibility and collaboration
between security and privacy.

Appendix: Privacy Risk Factors
This appendix provides seven factors to help organizations determine the privacy risk level
associated with the PII processed in NSS. The factors should be applied by following these
activities:

a. Consider the factors (in the Appendix) individually and select the corresponding
risk level of each factor.
To support consistency, organizations may not eliminate factors from the privacy risk
analysis. Additional factors may be added to determine a system’s privacy risk level in an
exceptional situation, but not as a matter of course.
b. Next, consider the risk levels of the factors collectively and determine the overall
privacy risk level of the PII, and by extension of the system.
The Privacy Overlays do not follow a high watermark approach for evaluating privacy
risk factors. Agencies may weight factors equally or weight them corresponding to the
specific circumstances or context of the PII or system. Additionally, if a factor is a higher
risk level than all other factors for the system, the agency could select the lower risk level
and tailor in supplemental controls that specifically address the risk of the outlying factor.
Appendix D provides system risk analysis use cases. The SAOP, or designee, must
approve the Privacy Overlays selected for the system.
c. Select the applicable control set – Low, Moderate, or High.
